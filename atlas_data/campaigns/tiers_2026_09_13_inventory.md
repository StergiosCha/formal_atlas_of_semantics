## Derrida, Signature Event Context — P0, ID172

Read the full local English text, *Limited Inc*, pp.1-23 including notes.
Ontology: marks, repeatable signifying forms, occurrences, contexts, authors,
receivers, intentions and effects. These are not supplied as mathematical
carriers or a decidable interpretation function. Form identity is only a
restricted stand-in for the essay's qualified recognition/repetition relation.

- D1, pp.7-10: readability/iterability beyond the presence of a particular
  producer or receiver is a structural possibility, not a guarantee of every
  actual reception. Attempt an illustrative occurrence/context model; label
  independence from origin metadata as a modeling choice.
- D2, pp.9,12: detachment and citational graft; an otherwise agrammatical
  phrase can function as an example of agrammaticality. Implement this worked
  contrast and unbounded syntactic quotation, without calling it a proof of
  semantic non-saturation.
- D3, p.12: this does NOT imply validity outside a context. Test that retained
  form does not determine uptake; also show that indefinitely many syntactic
  contexts alone does not force different meanings.
- D4, pp.19-20: repeatable signature form and singular signature event must
  not be conflated. A conditional non-recoverability theorem will show that
  indistinguishable forms cannot by themselves recover different origins.
  It is NOT a claim that authentication by richer evidence is impossible.
- D5, pp.15-18: possible failure coexists with successful performatives.
  Use a small explicit accessibility model to separate necessary possibility
  of failure from universal actual failure. The modal model is added machinery.
- D6, pp.3,9,18-21: universal non-saturation of context, dissemination,
  logocentrism and the philosophical critique of Austin are not established
  by any of these finite/structural models. Record as outside the formalized
  signature, not as false or inconsistent. Do not replace them with axioms.

## Austin, How to Do Things with Words — P1, ID177

Read the performative examples pp.5-11 and Lectures II-IV pp.12-52, focusing
on the six conditions and their qualifications. Ontology: conventional
procedures, participants/circumstances, executions, thoughts/intentions,
subsequent conduct, intended institutional acts and other effects.

- A1, pp.14-18: A1/A2/B1/B2 versus Gamma1/Gamma2. Gamma conditions apply
  where the procedure calls for the relevant attitudes/conduct. Encode a
  parameterized checklist, not an automatic test of social facts.
- A2, pp.16-17: misfire prevents the intended act, not all causal effects;
  abuse can accompany an achieved act. Keep intended achievement separate
  from the diagnostic checklist and from incidental effects. The text gives
  necessary conditions, not a sufficient complete decision procedure.
- A3, pp.11,16,39-41: an insincere promise is still a promise. Model this
  as a source example with achievement explicitly supplied, not inferred from
  a claim that all six checks are sufficient.
- A4, pp.9,36-37: a bet needs acceptance; correct execution and complete
  execution differ. A finite trace recognizer for the two-step example is
  an added implementation, not Austin's universal procedure grammar.
- A5, pp.23-24,26,28-29,38: failures can coexist and alternative classifications
  can overlap. Return all failed conditions, not one exclusive class.
- A6, pp.21-22,31,37-38: exclusions, vague applicability, uptake and procedural
  laxness remain real limitations. Model unknown assessments without silently
  converting them to success. Exhibit that satisfying listed conditions does
  not settle felicity in a model with another excluded condition.
- A7, pp.41-43,49-50: sincerity is not factual correctness; assertion/belief
  mismatch is not a logical contradiction. Include a countercase, not a theorem
  that utterance truth guarantees sincerity.

## Horn, Toward a New Taxonomy for Pragmatic Inference — P2, ID67

Read sections 1-11, pp.11-31. Focus: section 3 Q/R (p.13), section 7 (12)
(pp.20-21), section 8 (16)-(18) (pp.22-23), and section 10 examples and limits
(pp.26-29). Ontology: utterances, weaker/stronger contents, speaker knowledge,
relevance, alternative expressions, stereotypical situations and markedness.

- H1, p.13: not using a relevant stronger expression licenses lack of knowledge
  of that stronger content. A stronger conclusion that it is false needs
  additional epistemic premises. Implement the distinction and a countermodel
  to silently replacing ignorance by negative truth. Relevant/available
  alternatives and an observance premise remain explicit.
- H2, pp.13-15,18-19: Q upper-bounding versus R enrichment; context/contour
  can cancel either inference. Do not conjoin competing defaults automatically.
- H3, pp.20-21 (10)-(13): if S entails W, descriptive not-W entails not-S.
  Rejecting Q's upper bound while affirming S differs from descriptive negation.
  Test the numeral case and R-cancellation without descriptive negation.
  The logic alone does NOT prove the empirical claim that negation never
  receives an R-cancelling interpretation; keep that claim separate.
- H4, pp.22-23 (16)-(18): with an available corresponding unmarked expression,
  a marked expression tends to signal a non-stereotypical situation. Formalize
  the conditional partition and an executable finite interpreter. Stereotypes,
  correspondence, relevance and normal conditions are INPUTS, not inferred.
- H5, pp.27-29 (28)-(34): killed/caused to die and stopped/got to stop illustrate
  the partition. But a shorter synonym is not automatically a corresponding
  unmarked alternative; the author explicitly calls for refinement. Test
  disabled correspondence and identical lexical extensions with different
  pragmatic readings. No universal synonym-blocking law is claimed.
- H6: historical/empirical generalizations, full weighting of Q against R,
  learned stereotypes and grammaticalization are outside this implementation.

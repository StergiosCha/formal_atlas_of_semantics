## S2-01 — Political proxy and representational staging are not interchangeable

- **Claim, type, centrality:** Printed pp. 275–279. Vertretung (speaking for,
  political representation) and Darstellung (re-presentation, portrayal/staging)
  are related but discontinuous. Conflating them can support the misleading
  claim that representation has disappeared while intellectual mediation
  continues. This distinction organizes the critique, not merely terminology.
- **Source cases and limits:** The reading of Marx's small peasant proprietors
  distinguishes economic/descriptive class from political organization and
  its representative. The two senses of class must not become a contradiction
  on one unqualified predicate. The two representation practices are not
  independent sealed domains; the source insists on their complicity.
- **Possible obligation and limit:** A restricted model could distinguish
  proxy roles from portrayals and show that neither entails the other without
  a bridge premise, while allowing both in one practice. Merely declaring two
  constructors proves only a design choice; independence examples would not
  establish the source's historical or ideological diagnosis. Any link to the
  actual practices needs evidence outside the type declaration.
- **Parameters versus additions:** Agents, institutions, portrayals and proxy
  relations are representational inputs. Universal independence, a transparent
  subject, or a formal criterion of legitimate representation adds commitments
  the essay does not supply as mathematical definitions.
- **Input versus prediction:** The Marx interpretation and institutional roles
  would be sourced annotations, not consequences of generic relation algebra.
- **Robustness:** Not yet specified for the substantive complicity claim. Two
  data encodings of labels would be engineering equivalence, not semantic
  robustness of Spivak's argument.

## S2-02 — Epistemic violence cannot be reduced to an unmediated voice channel

- **Claim, type, centrality:** Printed pp. 271–275, 280–283, 285–287, 291–296.
  Intellectual and institutional practices help constitute what can count as
  the colonized subject and its knowledge. Declaring the intellectual merely
  transparent, or retrieving a supposedly pure consciousness behind records,
  fails to examine those practices. This is a historical/conceptual thesis,
  not a specified information-theoretic channel theorem.
- **Source cases and limits:** Colonial Hindu-law codification and education
  furnish the institutional examples; Macaulay's interpreter project is cited
  critically. Spivak states that she is not offering a privileged account of
  how things really were (p. 281). Archival traces are neither transparent
  access nor evidence of a pristine, wholly unrepresentable subject.
- **Possible obligation and limit:** No adequate mathematical obligation is
  fixed for the central thesis. An illustrative distinction among an event,
  its record, an interpretation and institutional uptake could prevent a
  category error, but would not derive epistemic violence. A lossy-message
  model would require an argued bridge, not be an automatic formalization.
- **Parameters versus additions:** Institutions, documents, translations and
  historical locations may be annotated inputs. Treating the subject as a
  pre-given signal, defining violence as dropped bits, or assuming the desired
  exclusion in a recognition function would add major semantic commitments.
- **Input versus prediction:** The codification/education history would be
  source evidence entered into a model, never something proved from its axioms.
- **Robustness:** Not yet specified; historical adequacy and interpretation
  require external review/evidence, not just two implementations.

## S2-03 — Subaltern positions are heterogeneous and historically located

- **Claim, type, centrality:** Printed pp. 281, 283–290 and note 59,
  pp. 311–312. Neither a pure knowing subaltern subject nor a uniform universal
  “Third World” follows from the essay. Positions can vary with region and
  material relations; gender, colonial production and international division
  of labor matter. This qualifies who the central argument concerns.
- **Source cases and limits:** Guha's elite/people stratification is quoted
  and interrogated: groups can be dominant in one area and dominated in
  another, with a complicated buffer zone. The definition of people as a
  demographic complement is **Guha's quoted scheme, not an unqualified
  definition adopted by Spivak**. India is not made representative of every
  other country, and local resistance is not denied merely because its
  relation to larger structures needs examination.
- **Possible obligation and limit:** A context-indexed relation could exhibit
  a change of dominance position and reject the inference that a single
  global Boolean classification is adequate. This would be an illustrative
  countermodel to an oversimplification, not proof of actual regional/class
  relations or a formal definition of subalternity.
- **Parameters versus additions:** Contexts, material relations and documentary
  classifications are inputs. Equating subalternity with a complement set,
  poverty, a fixed demographic identity or low network degree adds theory.
- **Input versus prediction:** A sourced position-change example is input;
  labeling groups in a finite table does not independently discover their
  social relations. The dependencies on S2-02 and S2-04 must remain explicit.
- **Robustness:** Not yet specified for a source-defensible theory of position;
  a context-keyed table and an isomorphic relation alone would be data checks.

## S2-04 — Competing widow-sacrifice discourses can both efface female agency

- **Claim, type, centrality:** Printed pp. 296–306. The imperial rescue account
  and the nativist account of women's voluntary sacrifice can interlock while
  failing to recover the women's subject positions. The essay traces this
  through legal, scriptural, economic and patriarchal practices. This sustained
  historical argument is central, not disposable background to a speech model.
- **Source cases and limits:** Colonial abolition discourse is examined
  alongside sanctioned-suicide rules, asymmetric widow/widower trajectories,
  property interests and the construction of choice. Spivak calls abolition
  admirable (p. 299) and explicitly disavows advocating widow killing (p. 301).
  The ritual was not universal. Note 78 (p. 313) distinguishes Brahmanical
  regulative norms from a description of everything that actually happened.
- **Possible obligation and limit:** No source-faithful central theorem is
  specified. A pair of institutional rule systems could illustrate different
  assignments of agency, but rules, their legitimacy and their operation in
  history would remain externally supplied. A truth table showing two labels
  exclude a third is not this argument; nor does it prove a historical absence
  of every possible countervoice.
- **Parameters versus additions:** Documents, legal/normative rules and their
  attributed interpretations are evidence inputs. A closed exhaustive world
  of two discourses, a Boolean consent test or a universal causal law of
  patriarchy would be substantive and potentially distorting additions.
- **Input versus prediction:** Historical examples are sourced testimony and
  interpretation, not outputs of a neutral axiomatization. A rule may express
  a norm without showing universal compliance or endorsement by the essay.
- **Robustness:** Not yet specified. A defensible comparison would first need
  historically warranted representations of the same institutional practices.

## S2-05 — Translating names does not transparently recover social reality

- **Claim, type, centrality:** Printed pp. 304–307. Philological and naming
  practices can participate in subject construction; a translation or a
  common-noun gloss of a proper name is not transparent sociological evidence.
  This is an important inferential part of S2-04, not an independent universal
  prohibition on learning from names.
- **Source cases and limits:** The essay examines disputed scriptural readings,
  the term sati and colonial translations of women's names. It offers the
  countercomparison of translated names of French philosophers or corporate
  directors producing a misleading portrait of their society. The goddess
  narrative does not by itself prove that a culture is feminist. Spivak marks
  her own philological reading as situated and contestable (also note 82).
- **Possible obligation and limit:** A narrowly stated non-entailment test
  could keep names/glosses fixed while varying social practices, showing that
  those linguistic inputs alone do not logically determine a specified social
  property. This would expose a missing bridge premise, not adjudicate the
  Sanskrit reading, prove a particular colonial motive, or invalidate every
  evidential inference involving names.
- **Parameters versus additions:** Name occurrences, proposed translations
  and source attribution are inputs. Treating glosses as direct observations
  of social practice, or making all translations arbitrary, would add claims.
- **Input versus prediction:** Historical translations must be quoted as
  attributed evidence; the constructed pair of models would be explicitly
  illustrative and not asserted to be two historical societies.
- **Robustness:** Not yet specified for the historical claim. Renaming symbols
  tests invariance of an abstraction only, not adequacy of the translations.

## S2-06 — The Bhaduri case concerns failed hearing/reading, not absent action

- **Claim, type, centrality:** Printed pp. 287, 307–308. The concluding case
  links an attempted intervention in an available social text to its subsequent
  reading through a familiar story. The essay says the subaltern as female
  cannot be heard or read and concludes “The subaltern cannot speak.” Retain
  the force of that conclusion without turning it into a universal inability
  to vocalize, act or leave traces, which the essay's own cases contradict.
  Its exact scope is an interpretive question, not a settled formal quantifier.
- **Source cases and limits:** Spivak reports Bhuvaneswari Bhaduri's death,
  political involvement, timing intended to displace a pregnancy explanation,
  and family responses that reinstated an illicit-love account. The body-as-
  rewriting reading is qualified by “perhaps” and “in this reading”; family
  connections are part of the stated evidential provenance. Do not collapse
  absence of pregnancy into logical proof that no illicit relationship existed.
  The essay acknowledges women's participation in insurgency (p. 287), and
  rejects treating the individual example as a general model of intervention
  (p. 307). No successful matched countercase is supplied here.
- **Possible obligation and limit:** A limited illustration may distinguish
  an act/trace, attributed intention, received interpretation and recognized
  political agency, showing their non-equivalence. Giving a receiver the
  misreading as input cannot prove the historical failure or the concluding
  thesis. No universal communication-impossibility theorem is specified.
- **Parameters versus additions:** Reported evidence and explicitly attributed
  interpretations are inputs. An omniscient intention oracle, a universal
  decoder, exhaustively closed audiences or a Boolean definition of recognized
  agency are added commitments requiring justification.
- **Input versus prediction:** The historical trajectory is sourced narrative;
  constructed transition rules could at most explore conditional possibilities.
  The timing of the act is not an algorithm or a procedure to reproduce.
- **Robustness:** Not yet specified. Clarifying the intended sense of hearing/
  reading needs semantic and historical review before alternative encodings
  could plausibly represent the same claim.

## S2-07 — Mediation does not release the intellectual from responsibility

- **Claim, type, centrality:** Printed pp. 285, 294–297, 307–308. The answer
  is not self-abdication, a pure lost origin, or the claim that representation
  has withered away. The female intellectual has a circumscribed task that
  must not be disowned. This normative/methodological conclusion depends on
  S2-01–06 and prevents reading the essay as a prohibition on any representation.
- **Source cases and limits:** Learning to speak to the historically muted
  subject and examining one's investigating position are contrasted with
  transparent speaking-for/listening claims. Individual cases can illuminate
  discourse without furnishing a general model of intervention. Neither more
  theory alone nor an essentialist recovery of origins is offered as a solution.
- **Possible obligation and limit:** No source-determined deontic calculus,
  intervention algorithm or universal action-selection rule is identified.
  Writing an obligation as an axiom would restate a commitment, not prove its
  justification. Keep this central normative remainder uncovered if necessary.
- **Parameters versus additions:** Situated roles and practices can be described.
  A complete ethics, utility objective or mechanically decidable legitimacy
  criterion would be a substantive new theory.
- **Input versus prediction:** Endorsed responsibilities are claims being
  examined, not independently derived facts simply because they are inputs.
- **Robustness:** Not yet specified; normative adequacy cannot be inferred
  from compilation or invariance under alternative data structures.

## Scope and next attempt

The inventory retains the headline thesis, historical case, representation
distinction and normative conclusion rather than selecting only the easiest
terminological fragment. It does not independently reconstruct every cited
argument of Marx, Foucault, Deleuze, Derrida or Freud, resolve the philological
disputes, or validate the historical archive. Those dependencies stay visible.

After Rosch and the source-resolved Hobbs attempt, any bounded Spivak attempt
must report all seven target dispositions. S2-01 and S2-05 suggest limited
non-entailment illustrations; they are not surrogates for S2-02, S2-04, S2-06
or S2-07. If no defensible formal statement is found for a central argument,
record that outcome without claiming impossibility in principle. No theorem
count can warrant “Spivak formalized.” Independent semantic review remains
pending; no alternative-encoding robustness has been tested.
